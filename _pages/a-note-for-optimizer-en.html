Part I · Classical gradient optimizers

Classical optimization algorithms · Principles and implementation

Gradient descent uses a local quadratic model to choose a step size. SGD estimates gradients from samples. Momentum uses past displacements, while Adam adapts the step size of each coordinate.

1. Gradient descent and local quadratic models

Let F(x)F(x) be the training objective. If ∇F\nabla F is LL-Lipschitz, integrating along the displacement dd gives a quadratic upper bound.

F(x+d)≤F(x)+⟨∇F(x),d⟩+L2‖d‖22.F(x+d)\le F(x)+\langle\nabla F(x),d\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\lVert d\rVert_2^2.

Minimize the bound over dd and substitute the minimizing displacement to obtain the update and the resulting decrease.

xt+1=xt−1L∇F(xt),F(xt+1)≤F(xt)−12L‖∇F(xt)‖22.x_{t+1}=x_t-\frac1L\nabla F(x_t),\qquad F(x_{t+1})\le F(x_t)-\frac1{2L}\lVert\nabla F(x_t)\rVert_2^2.

If FF is bounded below, summing the decreases bounds the average squared gradient norm. For a convex objective, the distance recurrence gives an objective gap of F(xt)−F⋆=O(1/t)F(x_t)-F^\star=O(1/t). For a μ\mu-strongly convex objective, the gap contracts by a factor of 1−μ/L1-\mu/L.

Consider a two-dimensional quadratic function.

F(x)=12(x12+100x22),∇F(x)=(x1,100x2).F(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x_1^2+100x_2^2),\qquad \nabla F(x)=(x_1,100x_2).

Set η=0.015\eta=0.015. The first coordinate is multiplied by 0.9850.985 at each step and contracts slowly. The second is multiplied by −0.5-0.5 and alternates across zero. For a positive-definite quadratic, a direction with curvature λ\lambda is multiplied by 1−ηλ1-\eta\lambda at each step. All directions contract when 0<η<2/λmax0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}.

2. SGD and minibatches

Let the empirical risk be F(x)=n−1∑i=1nfi(x)F(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n f_i(x). The number of samples used in each update gives three methods.

Method Gradient per step Main trade-off
Full-batch GD Compute all sample gradients at the same parameter point and average them The cost of each step grows with the size of the dataset
Single-sample SGD Take the gradient of one random sample Low cost per step and relatively high variance
Minibatch SGD Average the gradients of BB samples Using more samples reduces variance, and their gradients can be computed in parallel

Given xtx_t, draw BB samples independently and uniformly. The minibatch gradient satisfies 𝔼[gt∣xt]=∇F(xt)\mathbb E[g_t\mid x_t]=\nabla F(x_t). If the second moment of the single-sample gradient noise is at most σ2\sigma^2, independence makes the cross terms vanish.

𝔼[‖gt−∇F(xt)‖22∣xt]≤σ2B.\mathbb E[\lVert g_t-\nabla F(x_t)\rVert_2^2\mid x_t]\le \frac{\sigma^2}{B}.

Averaged iterates in convex SGD

Let FF be convex and let x⋆x^\star be a minimizer over a closed convex feasible set. The projected update is xt+1=Π𝒳(xt−ηgt)x_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal X}(x_t-\eta g_t). The nonexpansiveness of projection, unbiased gradients, and the second-moment bound 𝔼‖gt‖22≤G2\mathbb E\lVert g_t\rVert_2^2\le G^2 give a distance recurrence.

2η𝔼[F(xt)−F⋆]≤𝔼‖xt−x⋆‖22−𝔼‖xt+1−x⋆‖22+η2G2.2\eta\,\mathbb E[F(x_t)-F^\star] \le \mathbb E\lVert x_t-x^\star\rVert_2^2- \mathbb E\lVert x_{t+1}-x^\star\rVert_2^2+\eta^2G^2.

Summing the recurrence makes the distance terms telescope. By convexity, the averaged iterate x¯T=T−1∑t=0T−1xt\bar x_T=T^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}x_t satisfies the following upper bound on the objective gap.

𝔼[F(x¯T)−F⋆]≤D22ηT+ηG22,D=‖x0−x⋆‖2.\mathbb E[F(\bar x_T)-F^\star]\le \frac{D^2}{2\eta T}+\frac{\eta G^2}{2}, \qquad D=\lVert x_0-x^\star\rVert_2.

Setting η=D/(GT)\eta=D/(G\sqrt T) gives an expected objective gap of at most DG/TDG/\sqrt T at the averaged iterate.

Batch size and number of updates

For an unconstrained, smooth nonconvex objective, let F⋆F^\star be a lower bound. Set η≤1/L\eta\le 1/L and substitute the minibatch update into the descent bound from Section 1.

1T∑t=0T−1𝔼‖∇F(xt)‖22≤2(F(x0)−F⋆)ηT+Lησ2B.\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb E\lVert\nabla F(x_t)\rVert_2^2 \le \frac{2(F(x_0)-F^\star)}{\eta T}+\frac{L\eta\sigma^2}{B}.

With a fixed number of steps, increasing BB reduces the variance term. With a fixed sample budget N=BTN=BT, increasing BB also reduces the number of updates and therefore increases the first term.

3. Momentum and Nesterov acceleration

The heavy-ball displacement recurrence

Let dt=xt−xt−1d_t=x_t-x_{t-1}. Heavy-ball momentum carries forward the previous displacement.

dt+1=γdt−η∇F(xt),xt+1=xt+dt+1.d_{t+1}=\gamma d_t-\eta\nabla F(x_t),\qquad x_{t+1}=x_t+d_{t+1}.

The weights on older gradients decay as powers of γ\gamma. Gradients pointing in the same direction accumulate, while gradients with alternating signs partially cancel. For a one-dimensional quadratic with curvature λ\lambda, the recurrence is

xt+1=(1+γ−ηλ)xt−γxt−1.x_{t+1}=(1+\gamma-\eta\lambda)x_t-\gamma x_{t-1}.

The iterates converge to zero when both roots of the characteristic polynomial z2−(1+γ−ηλ)z+γz^2-(1+\gamma-\eta\lambda)z+\gamma lie inside the unit circle.

Nesterov's extrapolation point

Heavy-ball computes the gradient at xtx_t. Nesterov computes it at the extrapolated point yt=xt+βt(xt−xt−1)y_t=x_t+\beta_t(x_t-x_{t-1}) and uses the slope there to correct the displacement.

For a deterministic smooth convex objective, initialize x0=y0x_0=y_0 and q0=1q_0=1, then apply the following recurrence.

xt+1=yt−1L∇F(yt),qt+1=1+1+4qt22,x_{t+1}=y_t-\frac1L\nabla F(y_t),\qquad q_{t+1}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4q_t^2}}2,
βt=qt−1qt+1,yt+1=xt+1+βt(xt+1−xt).\beta_t=\frac{q_t-1}{q_{t+1}},\qquad y_{t+1}=x_{t+1}+\beta_t(x_{t+1}-x_t).

Let zt=qtyt−(qt−1)xtz_t=q_ty_t-(q_t-1)x_t. Then zt+1=zt−qt∇F(yt)/Lz_{t+1}=z_t-q_t\nabla F(y_t)/L. Adding the decrease in the objective to the change in squared distance cancels the gradient cross terms. Applying qt(qt−1)=qt−12q_t(q_t-1)=q_{t-1}^2 (t≥1t\ge1) gives a nonincreasing potential.

Φt+1=qt2L(F(xt+1)−F⋆)+12‖zt+1−x⋆‖22.\Phi_{t+1}=\frac{q_t^2}{L}(F(x_{t+1})-F^\star)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert z_{t+1}-x^\star\rVert_2^2.

Since qt≥(t+2)/2q_t\ge(t+2)/2, the output point satisfies F(xt)−F⋆≤2L‖x0−x⋆‖22/(t+1)2F(x_t)-F^\star\le 2L\lVert x_0-x^\star\rVert_2^2/(t+1)^2 (t≥1t\ge1).

4. AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adadelta

In sparse features or embedding tables, gradients occur at different frequencies across coordinates. Adaptive methods use a positive diagonal matrix HtH_t to adjust the step size of each coordinate.

mind{⟨gt,d⟩+12ηd⊤Htd},d=−ηHt−1gt,\min_d \left\{\langle g_t,d\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}d^\top H_t d\right\},\qquad d=-\eta H_t^{-1}g_t,

A larger diagonal entry gives a smaller step in that coordinate. All gradient squares, divisions, and maxima below are taken coordinatewise. The term ϵ>0\epsilon>0 stabilizes the denominator.

AdaGrad accumulates squared gradients

st=st−1+gt2,xt+1=xt−ηgtst+ϵ.s_t=s_{t-1}+g_t^2,\qquad x_{t+1}=x_t-\eta\,\frac{g_t}{\sqrt{s_t+\epsilon}}.

Suppose two coordinates both have a current gradient of 1 and have accumulated squared gradients of 100 and 1. Ignoring ϵ\epsilon, the step sizes are 0.1η0.1\eta and η\eta, respectively. When st>0s_t>0, the inequality below telescopes and can be used to derive a regret bound for online convex optimization.

gt2st=st−st−1st≤2(st−st−1).\frac{g_t^2}{\sqrt{s_t}} =\frac{s_t-s_{t-1}}{\sqrt{s_t}} \le 2(\sqrt{s_t}-\sqrt{s_{t-1}}).

The accumulator retains the effect of large early gradients, leading to smaller steps later.

RMSProp smooths squared gradients

vt=ρvt−1+(1−ρ)gt2,xt+1=xt−ηgtvt+ϵ.v_t=\rho v_{t-1}+(1-\rho)g_t^2,\qquad x_{t+1}=x_t-\eta\,\frac{g_t}{\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}}.

When the squared gradient stays below vtv_t, the moving average falls and the step-size coefficient rises. The effective memory is about 1/(1−ρ)1/(1-\rho) updates.

Adadelta smooths gradients and displacements

Use vtv_t to smooth squared gradients and utu_t to smooth squared displacements.

vt=ρvt−1+(1−ρ)gt2,dt=−ut−1+ϵvt+ϵgt,ut=ρut−1+(1−ρ)dt2.v_t=\rho v_{t-1}+(1-\rho)g_t^2,\qquad d_t=-\frac{\sqrt{u_{t-1}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}}g_t,\qquad u_t=\rho u_{t-1}+(1-\rho)d_t^2.

The parameter update is xt+1=xt+dtx_{t+1}=x_t+d_t. The original method uses the displacement RMS from the previous step in place of an explicit global learning rate.

5. Adam and its variants

Adam's moment estimates and bias correction

Initialize m0=v0=0m_0=v_0=0 and begin updating at t=1t=1.

mt=β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt,vt=β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2,m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\qquad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2,
m^t=mt1−β1t,v^t=vt1−β2t,xt+1=xt−ηtm^tv^t+ϵ.\widehat m_t=\frac{m_t}{1-\beta_1^t},\qquad \widehat v_t=\frac{v_t}{1-\beta_2^t},\qquad x_{t+1}=x_t-\eta_t\frac{\widehat m_t}{\sqrt{\widehat v_t}+\epsilon}.

Here mtm_t is the moving average of the gradients, and vtv_t is the moving average of their squares. If the gradient is constant at gg, starting from zero gives mt=(1−β1t)gm_t=(1-\beta_1^t)g. Bias correction restores the weight missing at early steps, with the same principle applying to the squared gradients. Ignoring ϵ\epsilon, the first update on a coordinate with a nonzero gradient is −η1sign(g1)-\eta_1\operatorname{sign}(g_1).

Nadam's numerator

Nadam replaces Adam's numerator m^t\widehat m_t with a combination of the current gradient and corrected momentum.

β1m^t+1−β11−β1tgt.\beta_1\widehat m_t+\frac{1-\beta_1}{1-\beta_1^t}g_t.

AdaMax's decaying maximum

The numerator is m^t\widehat m_t, and the denominator becomes ut+ϵu_t+\epsilon, where ut=max(β2ut−1,|gt|)u_t=\max(\beta_2u_{t-1},\lvert g_t\rvert). Once a gradient spike sets the maximum, its contribution is multiplied by β2\beta_2 at each step until a new gradient exceeds it.

AMSGrad's running maximum

When Adam's second moment decreases at a fixed learning rate, the coordinatewise step-size coefficient rises again. AMSGrad maintains v¯t=max(v¯t−1,vt)\bar v_t=\max(\bar v_{t-1},v_t) and uses v¯t\sqrt{\bar v_t} for scaling. The running maximum is nondecreasing, which lets the analysis bound changes in the metric across steps.

Decoupled weight decay in AdamW

In ordinary SGD, adding λxt\lambda x_t to the gradient is equivalent to multiplying the parameters directly by 1−ηtλ1-\eta_t\lambda. Adam's moment estimates absorb a regularizer added to the gradient. AdamW updates its moments using the loss gradient and scales the parameters separately.

xt+1=(1−ηtλ)xt−ηtm^tv^t+ϵ.x_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)x_t-\eta_t\frac{\widehat m_t}{\sqrt{\widehat v_t}+\epsilon}.

6. A two-dimensional quadratic experiment

The starting point is (4, 1), and heavy-ball momentum is 0.8. Choose a step size and iteration to see the objective values.

GD Heavy-ball Nesterov

On the quadratic from Section 1, all three methods start at (4,1)(4,1) and use η=1/100\eta=1/100. Heavy-ball uses γ=0.8\gamma=0.8, and Nesterov follows the schedule in Section 3. The code prints the iterates and objective values for each method.

from math import sqrt


def loss(x):
    return 0.5 * (x[0] ** 2 + 100 * x[1] ** 2)


def trace(method, steps=80, eta=0.01, gamma=0.8):
    x = prev = y = (4.0, 1.0)
    q = 1.0
    yield x
    for _ in range(steps):
        query = y if method == "Nesterov" else x
        g = (query[0], 100 * query[1])
        nxt = tuple(query[j] - eta * g[j] for j in range(2))
        if method == "Heavy-ball":
            nxt = tuple(nxt[j] + gamma * (x[j] - prev[j]) for j in range(2))
        if method == "Nesterov":
            q_next = (1 + sqrt(1 + 4 * q * q)) / 2
            beta = (q - 1) / q_next
            y = tuple(nxt[j] + beta * (nxt[j] - x[j]) for j in range(2))
            q = q_next
        prev, x = x, nxt
        yield x


for name in ("GD", "Heavy-ball", "Nesterov"):
    history = list(trace(name))
    for t in (1, 10, 40, 80):
        a, b = history[t]
        print(f"{name:10s} step={t:2d} x=({a:+.4f}, {b:+.4f}) F={loss(history[t]):.6f}")

At a step size of 0.010, GD sets the high-curvature coordinate to zero on the first step and multiplies the low-curvature coordinate by 0.99 each step. Heavy-ball also zeros the high-curvature coordinate on the first step, then momentum carries it across zero on the second. At 0.015, GD converges and this Nesterov recurrence diverges.

Part II · EMA and Nesterov extrapolation

From exponential moving averages to parameter extrapolation

An exponential moving average (EMA) smooths a sequence of observations using a single recursive value. Each new observation is blended with the previous average. Adam in Part I uses EMA to smooth gradients, while EMA-Nesterov uses it to smooth parameter displacements.

1. What is EMA?

Let ztz_t be observation tt and let sts_t be the smoothed value. Given an initial value s0s_0 and a smoothing coefficient 0≤γ<10\le\gamma<1, each update is

st=γst−1+(1−γ)zt.s_t=\gamma s_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)z_t.

For example, set γ=0.8\gamma=0.8 and s0=10s_0=10. The first two observations are 15 and 5. The EMA updates first to 0.8×10+0.2×15=110.8\times10+0.2\times15=11, then to 0.8×11+0.2×5=9.80.8\times11+0.2\times5=9.8.

Expanding the recurrence reveals the weight of each past observation.

st=γts0+(1−γ)∑j=1tγt−jzj.s_t=\gamma^t s_0+(1-\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{t}\gamma^{t-j}z_j.

A simple moving average gives equal weight to observations in its window. In an EMA, the weight is multiplied by γ\gamma for every step further back. When γ=0.8\gamma=0.8, the three most recent observations receive weights of 0.20, 0.16, and 0.128. Each update needs only the previous average.

Smoothing and lag

A larger γ\gamma gives less weight to new observations. The curve becomes smoother and responds more slowly to changes in trend. If observations alternate between +a+a and −a-a, the steady-state EMA amplitude is (1−γ)/(1+γ)(1-\gamma)/(1+\gamma) times the original amplitude. Under the steady-state weights, the mean age of an observation is γ/(1−γ)\gamma/(1-\gamma) steps. When γ=0.995\gamma=0.995, that mean is 199 steps.

2. Nesterov and other extrapolation methods

Classical Nesterov: take the gradient at an extrapolated point

Write dt=xt−xt−1d_t=x_t-x_{t-1}. Heavy-ball momentum takes the gradient at xtx_t and adds the displacement term βtdt\beta_t d_t. Nesterov first extrapolates along the displacement and then takes the gradient at yty_t.

yt=xt+βtdt,xt+1=yt−ht∇f(yt).y_t=x_t+\beta_t d_t,\qquad x_{t+1}=y_t-h_t\nabla f(y_t).

For an LL-smooth convex objective, use exact gradients, ht=1/Lh_t=1/L, and βt=t/(t+3)\beta_t=t/(t+3). The objective gap is bounded above by O(T−2)O(T^{-2}). In minibatch training, extrapolation carries oscillations from the last displacement into the query point.

Lookahead: interpolate between the endpoints of the inner loop

Starting from the slow parameters ϕt−1\phi_{t-1}, the base optimizer takes KK consecutive steps to reach the fast parameters θt,K\theta_{t,K}. Then update the slow parameters.

ϕt=(1−a)ϕt−1+aθt,K,0<a<1.\phi_t=(1-a)\phi_{t-1}+a\theta_{t,K},\qquad 0<a<1.

The new slow parameters lie between the endpoints and become the starting point of the next round. The sum of the displacements over KK steps determines their update direction.

NALA: take an additional gradient step after the inner loop

Run KK steps from ϕt−1\phi_{t-1} to obtain the fast parameters θt,K\theta_{t,K}. Use the full displacement to form the query point yty_t, then take an outer gradient step.

yt=θt,K+μN(θt,K−ϕt−1),ϕt=yt−a∇f(yt).y_t=\theta_{t,K}+\mu_N(\theta_{t,K}-\phi_{t-1}),\qquad \phi_t=y_t-a\nabla f(y_t).

When μN>0\mu_N>0, the query point is beyond the endpoint. When −1<μN<0-1<\mu_N<0, it lies between the endpoints. The value μN=−0.5\mu_N=-0.5 used in the NALA experiments gives the midpoint.

SNOO: apply momentum to each inner-loop displacement

Let FtF_t denote KK consecutive base updates. Starting from the outer parameters wtw_t, the inner loop ends at ut=Ft(wt)u_t=F_t(w_t) and gives the pseudogradient st=wt−uts_t=w_t-u_t. Initialize b−1=0b_{-1}=0 and update the outer momentum once per inner loop.

bt=μbt−1+st,wt+1=wt−η(st+μbt).b_t=\mu b_{t-1}+s_t,\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta(s_t+\mu b_t).

When μ=0\mu=0, the outer update reduces to Lookahead interpolation. Set xt=wt+ημbt−1x_t=w_t+\eta\mu b_{t-1} and yt=wty_t=w_t to obtain xt+1−xt=−ηbtx_{t+1}-x_t=-\eta b_t. It follows that

yt=xt+μ(xt−xt−1),xt+1=(1−η)yt+ηFt(yt),y_t=x_t+\mu(x_t-x_{t-1}),\qquad x_{t+1}=(1-\eta)y_t+\eta F_t(y_t),

Here x−1=x0x_{-1}=x_0. The inner loop starts at the extrapolated point yty_t, and the original outer parameters are wt=ytw_t=y_t.

GPA: control gradient query points and evaluation parameters separately

Generalized Primal Averaging (GPA) maintains three sets of parameters. The base update acts on ztz_t, xtx_t smooths past parameter values and is used for evaluation, and yty_t is where the gradient is evaluated. With SGD as the base update, initialize x0=z0x_0=z_0.

yt=μyxt+(1−μy)zt,zt+1=zt−htgt,y_t=\mu_y x_t+(1-\mu_y)z_t,\qquad z_{t+1}=z_t-h_t g_t,
xt+1=μxxt+(1−μx)zt+1,gt=∇f(yt;ξt).x_{t+1}=\mu_x x_t+(1-\mu_x)z_{t+1},\qquad g_t=\nabla f(y_t;\xi_t).

The coefficient μx\mu_x controls the smoothing of the evaluation parameters, and μy\mu_y controls the location of the query point. Setting them equal gives Nesterov's primal averaging form. Substituting the equations above into the difference between query points gives

yt+1−yt=(1−μy)(zt+1−zt)+μy(1−μx)(zt+1−xt).y_{t+1}-y_t=(1-\mu_y)(z_{t+1}-z_t)+\mu_y(1-\mu_x)(z_{t+1}-x_t).

The first term comes from the most recent update. The second comes from the distance between the new parameters and their historical average.

3. The EMA-Nesterov update rule

EMA-Nesterov feeds each displacement dt=xt−xt−1d_t=x_t-x_{t-1} into the EMA and uses the smoothed displacement mtm_t to form the extrapolated point.

Initialize m0=0m_0=0. Let 𝒜t\mathcal A_t denote one update of the base optimizer, γ∈[0,1)\gamma\in[0,1) the smoothing coefficient, and βt≥0\beta_t\ge 0 the extrapolation coefficient.

yt=xt+βtmt,xt+1=𝒜t(yt),y_t=x_t+\beta_t m_t,\qquad x_{t+1}=\mathcal A_t(y_t),
mt+1=γmt+(1−γ)(xt+1−xt).m_{t+1}=\gamma m_t+(1-\gamma)(x_{t+1}-x_t).

The base optimizer evaluates the gradient at yty_t and carries forward its own state. With SGD, the parameter displacement that updates the EMA is the sum of the extrapolation term and the gradient step.

xt+1−xt=βtmt−αt∇f(yt;ξt).x_{t+1}-x_t=\beta_t m_t-\alpha_t\nabla f(y_t;\xi_t).

When γ=0\gamma=0, mtm_t is the most recent displacement. When βt=0\beta_t=0, the base update starts from xtx_t, and the new displacement is still recorded.

4. Convergence rates in convex optimization

Both theorems use exact gradients and a base step size of 1/L1/L, so 𝒜(y)=y−∇f(y)/L\mathcal A(y)=y-\nabla f(y)/L. Write x⋆x^\star for the minimizer and f⋆f^\star for the optimal value.

Strongly convex objectives

Suppose ff is LL-smooth and μ\mu-strongly convex, with κ=L/μ>1\kappa=L/\mu>1. Choose 0≤γ<1−1/κ0\le\gamma<1-1/\kappa and set

r=1−γκ,βt=(1−γ−1/κ)2(1−γ)(1−1/κ).r=\sqrt{\frac{1-\gamma}{\kappa}},\qquad \beta_t=\frac{(\sqrt{1-\gamma}-\sqrt{1/\kappa})^2}{(1-\gamma)(1-1/\kappa)}.

Let vt=xt+1−γ−r(1−γ)rmtv_t=x_t+\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r}m_t. The potential Et=f(xt)−f⋆+μ2‖vt−x⋆‖22E_t=f(x_t)-f^\star+\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert v_t-x^\star\rVert_2^2 satisfies Et+1≤(1−r)EtE_{t+1}\le(1-r)E_t, so

f(xT)−f⋆≤(1−r)TE0.f(x_T)-f^\star\le (1-r)^T E_0.

The choice γ=0\gamma=0 gives the classical Nesterov contraction factor 1−1/κ1-1/\sqrt{\kappa}. Increasing γ\gamma makes the upper bound contract more slowly.

General convex objectives

Let ff be an LL-smooth convex function, with 0≤γ<10\le\gamma<1. Set

ct=1+γ4(1−γ)+t4,βt=ct−γct+11+(1−γ)ct+1.c_t=1+\frac{\gamma}{4(1-\gamma)}+\frac{t}{4},\qquad \beta_t=\frac{c_t-\gamma c_{t+1}}{1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}}.

The proof combines the objective gap with the squared distance from an auxiliary point xt+ctmtx_t+c_t m_t in a potential. The weight on the objective gap grows with t2t^2, giving

f(xT)−f⋆≤97L‖x0−x⋆‖222(1−γ)3T2.f(x_T)-f^\star \le\frac{97L\lVert x_0-x^\star\rVert_2^2}{2(1-\gamma)^3 T^2}.

For fixed γ\gamma, the objective gap decays at rate T−2T^{-2}. The coefficient in the upper bound grows as γ\gamma approaches 1.

5. Scheduling extrapolation during training

The example below uses f(x)=x2/2f(x)=x^2/2 to show the order of operations. The base update takes the extrapolated point and returns the updated parameters.

def ema_nesterov(x, base_step, betas, gamma):
    momentum = 0.0
    for t, beta in enumerate(betas):
        y = x + beta * momentum
        x_next = base_step(y, t)
        momentum = gamma * momentum + (1 - gamma) * (x_next - x)
        x = x_next
    return x


def gradient_step(y, t):
    return y - 0.1 * y


betas = [0.0] * 3 + [0.5] * 12 + [0.0] * 5
result = ema_nesterov(4.0, gradient_step, betas, gamma=0.9)
print(f"x = {result:.6f}, loss = {0.5 * result**2:.6f}")

Part III · The Muon family and heavy-tailed noise

AdamW adjusts the update magnitude elementwise. Muon operates on the whole weight matrix and changes the singular values of its momentum matrix. This determines how Muon updates weights, why it needs matrix multiplication, and how it can be sharded across GPUs.

1. Muon's origins, recurrence, and implementation

Why Muon operates on singular values

Keller Jordan and colleagues introduced Muon in their 2024 work on speeding up NanoGPT. Its full name is MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton–Schulz. They observed that Transformer matrix updates often concentrate in a few singular directions. Muon starts from SGD momentum and narrows the gap between the magnitudes of the update in different singular directions.

Suppose the momentum matrix is B=diag(12,1)B=\operatorname{diag}(12,1). Updating directly with momentum gives a 12-to-1 magnitude ratio between the two directions. Muon's idealized transform changes it to diag(1,1)\operatorname{diag}(1,1). For a general matrix, write its singular value decomposition B=UΣV⊤B=U\Sigma V^\top, retain the left and right singular vectors associated with nonzero singular values, and set Q=UV⊤Q=UV^\top. This sets all retained singular values to 1. The matrix QQ is called the polar factor.

Why constrain the spectral norm?

In a linear layer WxWx, a weight change DD changes the output by DxDx. The spectral norm ‖D‖op\lVert D\rVert_{\rm op} of DD is the largest possible output change over unit inputs, equivalently the largest singular value of DD. Constraining the spectral norm of the update therefore bounds its effect on every unit input.

When momentum BB estimates the descent direction, the first-order term is ⟨B,D⟩F\langle B,D\rangle_F. The duality inequality gives the lower bound −‖B‖∗-\lVert B\rVert_*, the negative sum of the singular values. Choosing D=−QD=-Q attains this bound.

−Q∈argmin‖D‖op≤1⟨B,D⟩F.-Q\in\arg\min_{\lVert D\rVert_{\rm op}\le 1}\langle B,D\rangle_F.
⟨B,−Q⟩F=−‖B‖∗.\langle B,-Q\rangle_F=-\lVert B\rVert_*.

One update in practice

Let Wt∈ℝm×nW_t\in\mathbb R^{m\times n} and Gt=∇Wℓ(Wt;ξt)G_t=\nabla_W\ell(W_t;\xi_t). Jordan's implementation first adds the current gradient to the momentum MtM_t, then mixes the current gradient with the updated momentum to form BtB_t, and finally applies kk Newton–Schulz iterations to BtB_t.

The default momentum coefficient is 0.95. With Nesterov-style mixing disabled, use Bt=MtB_t=M_t. The weight update first scales down the old weights and then subtracts the scaled matrix direction.

Implementing Newton–Schulz on GPUs

The GPU implementation approximates the polar factor with matrix multiplications. First scale the input by its Frobenius norm so its spectral norm is at most 1, taking X0=Bt/(‖Bt‖F+ϵ)X_0=B_t/(\lVert B_t\rVert_F+\epsilon). If the matrix has more rows than columns, transpose it before the iteration and transpose it back afterward. This makes the side length of Aj=XjXj⊤A_j=X_jX_j^\top the smaller matrix dimension.

Aj=XjXj⊤,Xj+1=aXj+(bAj+cAj2)Xj.A_j=X_jX_j^\top,\qquad X_{j+1}=aX_j+(bA_j+cA_j^2)X_j.

Substituting the singular value decomposition of XjX_j shows that AjA_j corresponds to squared singular values, AjXjA_jX_j to cubes, and Aj2XjA_j^2X_j to fifth powers. Each iteration preserves the left and right singular vectors and maps each singular value zz to az+bz3+cz5az+bz^3+cz^5. Jordan's implementation typically uses five iterations in BF16 with coefficients (3.4445,−4.7750,2.0315)(3.4445,-4.7750,2.0315). Applying the FP32 example below to diag(12,1)\operatorname{diag}(12,1) gives singular values of about 0.856 and 0.700 after five iterations. Both singular values of the ideal polar factor are 1.

The NumPy example below shows the same matrix recurrence in FP32.

import numpy as np


def muon_ns(matrix, steps=5):
    x = np.asarray(matrix, dtype=np.float32)
    transposed = x.shape[0] > x.shape[1]
    if transposed:
        x = x.T
    x = x / (np.linalg.norm(x) + 1e-7)
    for _ in range(steps):
        gram = x @ x.T
        x = 3.4445 * x + (-4.7750 * gram + 2.0315 * (gram @ gram)) @ x
    return x.T if transposed else x


def muon_step(weight, grad, momentum, lr, decay=0.0, beta=0.95):
    rows, cols = weight.shape
    momentum = beta * momentum + (1 - beta) * grad
    direction = muon_ns((1 - beta) * grad + beta * momentum)
    scale = np.sqrt(max(1.0, rows / cols))
    updated = (1 - lr * decay) * weight - lr * scale * direction
    return updated, momentum

Parameter grouping, shape scaling, and sharding

Parameter grouping. Jordan's recipe uses Muon for hidden-layer weight matrices and AdamW for input embeddings, output heads, normalization parameters, and biases. To apply Muon to a convolution kernel, flatten its last three dimensions.

Shape scaling. A full-rank polar factor has min(m,n)\min(m,n) singular values equal to 1, giving an elementwise root mean square (RMS) of 1/max(m,n)1/\sqrt{\max(m,n)}. Jordan's implementation uses sm,n=max(1,m/n)s_{m,n}=\sqrt{\max(1,m/n)} to bring the RMS row norm to 1. Moonlight uses sm,n=0.2max(m,n)s_{m,n}=0.2\sqrt{\max(m,n)} to bring the elementwise RMS to 0.2, comparable to the magnitude of AdamW updates. The finite-iteration approximation changes this value somewhat.

Multi-GPU training. Jordan's DDP implementation assigns different matrices to different GPUs and then gathers the updated weights. Moonlight shards the master weights and momentum across each data-parallel group. Each GPU updates momentum on its local gradient shard, then gathers the full matrix needed for orthogonalization. After computing the full update, each GPU applies only its local slice, and the weights are synchronized. The gather is needed because XX⊤XX^\top uses data from the whole matrix.

Moonlight used this recipe to train an MoE with 16B total parameters and about 3B active per token on 5.7T tokens.

2. MONA and gradient differences before orthogonalization

What gradient differences tell us

Consider steps of equal length. The greater the curvature of the loss along the step direction, the faster the gradient typically changes. For full gradients, the difference between successive gradients can be approximated by applying the Hessian to the displacement. MONA obtains this signal by subtracting two gradients it already has. Changing the minibatch also changes the gradient, so MONA smooths the difference with an EMA before adding it to the current gradient.

3. Controlling weight row norms with Muown

Why the weight matrix's spectral norm grows

Even when every update is bounded, many updates can accumulate into large weights. The spectral norm can grow because rows get longer or because their directions align more closely. For example, two orthogonal unit rows give a spectral norm of 1. Rotating them into the same direction leaves both row norms at 1 and raises the spectral norm to 2\sqrt 2.

Lion and colleagues separate these effects with an identity. Write each row as its length ai=‖Wi‖2a_i=\lVert W_i\rVert_2 times a unit direction UiU_i. Then use the diagonal matrix P=Diag(a/‖a‖∞)P=\operatorname{Diag}(a/\lVert a\rVert_\infty) to record each row's length relative to the longest row.

‖W‖op=‖a‖∞λmax(PUU⊤P).\lVert W\rVert_{\rm op}=\lVert a\rVert_\infty\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(P UU^\top P)}.

The first factor on the right is the maximum row norm. The second captures both how closely the row directions align and how long the rows are relative to one another. In a 500M experiment with weight decay disabled, the maximum row norm and spectral norm grew together. The second factor was fairly stable after a brief change early in training. When the authors fixed each row norm at its initial value, the systematic increase in spectral norm disappeared. Muown therefore updates each row's length in a separate branch.

Update each row's length and direction separately

Write row ii of the effective weight matrix as Wt,i=gt,iUt,iW_{t,i}=g_{t,i}U_{t,i}. The gain is gt,ig_{t,i}, so the actual row norm is |gt,i|\lvert g_{t,i}\rvert. The unit direction Ut,iU_{t,i} is obtained by dividing the internal variable Rt,iR_{t,i} by its norm rt,i=‖Rt,i‖2r_{t,i}=\lVert R_{t,i}\rVert_2. At initialization, use the original weights for RR and their row norms for gg and rr.

Backpropagation gives the effective weight gradient GtG_t. The component ht,ih_{t,i} along the current row direction updates the gain. Subtracting that component from Gt,iG_{t,i} leaves a perpendicular component that rotates the direction.

ht,i=⟨Gt,i,Ut,i⟩,Ht,i=gt,irt,i(Gt,i−ht,iUt,i).h_{t,i}=\langle G_{t,i},U_{t,i}\rangle,\qquad H_{t,i}=\frac{g_{t,i}}{r_{t,i}}(G_{t,i}-h_{t,i}U_{t,i}).

For example, let the current direction be (1,0)(1,0), the incoming gradient (2,4)(2,4), the gain gt,i=3g_{t,i}=3, and the internal row norm rt,i=2r_{t,i}=2. 5. Muon under heavy-tailed noise

Motivation: comparing Muon and Euclidean methods under large gradient noise

A minibatch gradient equals the true gradient plus sampling error. An occasional large error can contribute heavily to the variance when squared. Suppose, for example, that the probability of an absolute error of 2k2^k is proportional to 4−k4^{-k}. Each scale contributes equally to the second moment, and their sum diverges. For the 1.5th moment, each contribution decays as 2−k/22^{-k/2}, so the sum is finite. Hübler, Pethick, and Sra use this finite lower-order moment assumption to study Muon.

The comparison also needs a definition of being near a stationary point. Given a unit step budget for the update matrix DD, the maximum first-order decrease under the current gradient GG is the dual norm of that gradient. Euclidean methods constrain DD in Frobenius norm, giving a decrease of ‖G‖F\lVert G\rVert_F. Muon constrains DD in spectral norm, giving the nuclear-norm decrease ‖G‖∗\lVert G\rVert_*. The nuclear norm sums the singular values. If the gradient has 100 equal singular values, its nuclear norm is 10 times its Frobenius norm. At the same ε\varepsilon, the nuclear-norm criterion is stricter.

The paper uses a common target for both approaches. It analyzes nuclear-norm stationarity directly for Muon. For existing Euclidean guarantees, it first derives how small the Frobenius norm must be to imply the same nuclear-norm accuracy. It then compares the number of stochastic gradient calls needed to reach that target.

Assumptions and update rule

Let F(W)F(W) be the population loss, and let GtG_t be a stochastic gradient sampled at WtW_t. Write Zt=Gt−∇F(Wt)Z_t=G_t-\nabla F(W_t) for the sampling error. Assume unbiased stochastic gradients at every step and an initial loss within Δ1\Delta_1 of the lower bound. At every query point, require

𝔼‖Zt‖∗p≤σp,1<p≤2.\mathbb E\lVert Z_t\rVert_*^p\le\sigma^p,\qquad 1<p\le 2.

Here σ\sigma is the noise scale measured in nuclear norm and pp is the assumed finite moment order. The case p=2p=2 gives a bounded second moment. The constant LL bounds how quickly the true gradient changes. Multiplying the spectral norm of a weight displacement by LL bounds the nuclear norm of the gradient change.

In the analysis, Muon accumulates gradients in an EMA and steps along the exact polar factor of the momentum matrix. The finite Newton–Schulz iteration in Section 1 approximates this direction in practice.

Mt=βMt−1+(1−β)Gt.M_t=\beta M_{t-1}+(1-\beta)G_t.
Wt+1=Wt−ηQ(Mt).W_{t+1}=W_t-\eta Q(M_t).

How the proof handles heavy-tailed noise

If MtM_t equals the true gradient, the linearized decrease is η‖∇F(Wt)‖∗\eta\lVert\nabla F(W_t)\rVert_*. In practice, the method uses an estimated momentum. Let et=‖Mt−∇F(Wt)‖∗e_t=\lVert M_t-\nabla F(W_t)\rVert_*. The loss after one step satisfies

F(Wt+1)−F(Wt)≤−η‖∇F(Wt)‖∗+2ηet+Lη22.F(W_{t+1})-F(W_t) \le -\eta\lVert\nabla F(W_t)\rVert_*+2\eta e_t+\frac{L\eta^2}{2}.

Momentum error has two sources. Each sample introduces noise, which can be reduced by averaging more gradients. A longer window also retains more old gradients, even as the parameters move to a new point. Let δ=1−β\delta=1-\beta, so the window length is on the order of 1/δ1/\delta. Each step moves by at most η\eta, which makes the error from old gradients of order Lη/δL\eta/\delta.

The stochastic noise is harder to bound. The nuclear norm has kinks, making it difficult to apply moment inequalities directly to signed sums of noise. For example, adding δ\delta or −δ-\delta to the second diagonal entry of diag(1,0)\operatorname{diag}(1,0) changes its nuclear norm from 1 to 1+|δ|1+\lvert\delta\rvert in either case. The paper instead averages in the Schatten-pp norm. It applies the vector pp norm to the singular values and uses the pp-order moment to bound the accumulated noise. Only then does it convert back to nuclear norm using the inequality below, where d=min(m,n)d=\min(m,n).

‖Z‖∗≤d1−1/p‖Z‖Sp.\lVert Z\rVert_*\le d^{1-1/p}\lVert Z\rVert_{S_p}.

When p=1.5p=1.5, the conversion factor is d1/3d^{1/3}. Converting from the Frobenius norm would give d1/2d^{1/2}. The Schatten-pp norm balances these demands. It lets the proof use the available pp-order moment to analyze random sums while staying closer to the nuclear norm than the Frobenius norm does. The algorithm still uses the spectral norm to choose its direction. The Schatten-pp norm is used in the proof.

Main result: where the dimension savings come from

The leading error term for the EMA-weighted noise is of order σ(dδ)(p−1)/p\sigma(d\delta)^{(p-1)/p}. To bring it down to ε\varepsilon, choose δ\delta on the order of (ε/σ)p/(p−1)/d(\varepsilon/\sigma)^{p/(p-1)}/d. To keep the stale-gradient error Lη/δL\eta/\delta at order ε\varepsilon as well, choose a step size of order εδ/L\varepsilon\delta/L. After summing over TT steps, the initial loss gap contributes Δ1/(ηT)\Delta_1/(\eta T) per step. Reducing this term to ε\varepsilon gives the leading query complexity.

T=O(dΔ1Lε2(σε)p/(p−1)).T=O\left(d\frac{\Delta_1 L}{\varepsilon^2} \left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{p/(p-1)}\right).

The theorem bounds the mean expected nuclear norm of the gradients over TT steps. Equivalently, if an iterate is drawn uniformly from those steps, its expected gradient nuclear norm is at most ε\varepsilon. Each call means sampling one stochastic gradient. The GPU cost per update is a separate calculation.

For the Euclidean approach, substitute the accuracy implied by ‖G‖∗≤d‖G‖F\lVert G\rVert_*\le\sqrt d\,\lVert G\rVert_F. To reach nuclear-norm accuracy ε\varepsilon, set the Frobenius-norm accuracy to ε/d\varepsilon/\sqrt d. The existing Euclidean upper bound depends on accuracy as ε−(2+p/(p−1))\varepsilon^{-(2+p/(p-1))}. Substituting the tighter tolerance gives the dimension factor d(3p−2)/(2(p−1))d^{(3p-2)/(2(p-1))} for the Euclidean approach. Can the linear dependence on dd be removed? The first lower-bound construction hides unknown information in dd directions. Each call has a small chance of revealing the sign of one direction, and greater noise makes useful information less likely. Over the theorem's accuracy range, a stochastic first-order method needs at least on the order of d(σ/ε)p/(p−1)d(\sigma/\varepsilon)^{p/(p-1)} calls in the worst case. The second construction embeds several hard subproblems in the matrix. When its larger dimension reaches dΔ1L/ε2d\Delta_1 L/\varepsilon^2 and the target accuracy is sufficiently high, zero-respecting algorithms face a lower bound of the same order as the leading upper bound. Zero-respecting means iterates acquire nonzero components only in coordinates that past gradients have touched.

General result: choices beyond the spectral norm

The analysis also applies to other normalized steepest-descent methods with momentum. If the dual norm has a dimension-independent uniform smoothness constant of order pp, the sum of noise terms can be bounded directly in that norm, with no extra dimension factor in the leading query complexity. For matrices, take 2≤r≤p/(p−1)2\le r\le p/(p-1) and constrain the update with the Schatten-rr norm to obtain this type of guarantee.

For example, when p=1.5p=1.5, choose r=3r=3, whose dual is exactly the Schatten-1.5 norm. This stationarity criterion is stronger than the Frobenius criterion, and the theory still bounds noise directly in that norm. The spectral norm is the limit as rr tends to infinity. Its dual is the nuclear norm, which calls for the conversion above. Optimizer design therefore involves choosing the update constraint, stationarity criterion, and noise analysis together.

Takeaways

  1. Use the same stationarity criterion for the same numerical tolerance. For matrix gradients, the Frobenius and nuclear norms can differ by a factor of d\sqrt d. Matching the target accuracy shows which term accounts for the optimizer's savings in gradient calls.
  2. Different parameter groups can use different update geometries. The paper finds different noise ratios for hidden-layer matrices and embeddings. The ratio within a layer also changes over training. To investigate why Muon may work better for a particular layer, measure its noise scale and singular-value distribution at different training stages.
  3. Compare both the cost per step and the number of steps needed. In these experiments, intermediate Schatten geometries use a full SVD and attain lower perplexity, while spectral-norm updates are approximated with matrix multiplications. A practical comparison can fix a target validation loss and report both tokens consumed and training time.
